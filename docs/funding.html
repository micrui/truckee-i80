How the freeway is paid for

Who pays for the interstate over Donner Pass, what it costs to keep open, and how the wear is distributed, from federal spending records, Caltrans project documents, and the federal government's own cost studies.

California's truck weight fees go to bond debt, not roads

California collects roughly $1 billion a year in commercial vehicle weight fees. Since 2011, state law has directed that revenue away from the highway account and toward debt service on past transportation bonds.

Sources: California Transportation Commission funding overview · Legislative Analyst's Office

Who causes the wear

The federal government's pavement engineering rests on a finding from the AASHO Road Test: pavement damage rises with roughly the fourth power of axle weight. Doubling the load on an axle multiplies its pavement damage about sixteen times, which is why a loaded five-axle tractor-trailer consumes pavement on the order of several thousand cars.

Applied to this corridor's own counts, that relationship is decisive. The 2024 census puts roughly 30,000 cars and light vehicles and about 4,600 five-axle combinations a day through Truckee. If each loaded combination wears pavement like several thousand cars, the car traffic amounts to a rounding error: the truck fleet causes effectively all of the corridor's pavement wear. Caltrans' own documents agree without the arithmetic: its 2023 field review called the rutting damage unprecedented and attributed it to heavy trucks and chain wear, and the repair bill in the table above followed.

Payment runs on a different scale than wear. The federal Highway Cost Allocation Study puts both dimensions in dollars for the year 2000: each class's share of the costs it was assigned, and its share of the user fees actually paid.

Even these columns understate the pavement story, because "assigned costs" cover the whole federal program, including the new-capacity and enhancement spending where passenger vehicles dominate. For the slice that wears out under load, the study is blunter: combination trucks are responsible for over 40 percent of system preservation costs while making up about four percent of the nation's travel. Per mile, the study puts an automobile's cost responsibility at 0.65 cents and the heaviest combinations' at 16.6 cents, twenty-five times more.

The trucks on this corridor

The 2024 Caltrans truck census counts about 6,500 trucks a day through Truckee (roughly one vehicle in six), and about seven in ten of them are five-axle combinations, the heaviest common class. Caltrans describes 6,200 commercial vehicles a day crossing Donner Summit and values the goods they carry at more than $4.7 million per hour.

The same two dimensions can be put side by side for this corridor. By the fourth-power relationship, the truck fleet causes effectively all of the pavement wear here and the car fleet a few percent at most. On the payment side, at typical fuel economy (around 25 miles per gallon for cars, around 6 for loaded combinations) a car pays about 3 cents per mile in combined state and federal fuel tax and a combination truck about 12. At Truckee's traffic mix that works out to trucks paying a little under half of the fuel tax collected on this segment, against effectively all of the wear.

Sources: Caltrans Traffic Census, truck AADT tables · Caltrans release 19-213 · EPA Automotive Trends (fleet fuel economy) · DOE Alternative Fuels Data Center (truck fuel economy)
