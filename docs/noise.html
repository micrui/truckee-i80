The noise

What the freeway sounds like in town, measured; who makes the sound, by the state's own acoustics; and which rules govern it. The Town's general plan calls Interstate 80 "the loudest source of noise affecting the Town of Truckee."

The Town's modeling from those measurements puts the freeway's 60-decibel contour 2,700 feet from the roadway through central Truckee, and several hundred feet further at buildout. For scale: the federal criterion that triggers noise abatement analysis for homes is 67 decibels; the World Health Organization's health-based recommendation for road traffic is 53. The overnight measurements matter too: at the Coldstream site, night hours ran within a few decibels of daytime, because the traffic that runs all night is disproportionately trucks.

Who makes the sound

Caltrans' own acoustics manual states the equivalence plainly: at 55 miles per hour, one heavy truck makes as much noise as about ten cars, and one medium truck about four. Applying those published equivalences to the 2024 traffic counts at Truckee:

Cars and light vehicles (≈30,500)82.3%≈ 35%
Two-axle trucks (≈1,650)4.5%≈ 7%
Trucks with three or more axles (≈4,900)13.2%≈ 58%

The pattern repeats from the wear tables: less than a fifth of the vehicles produce about two-thirds of the sound energy. Grades sharpen it: on sustained climbs the federal model shifts heavy trucks toward full-throttle levels, and this corridor is one long climb in each direction.

Energy is not annoyance

The ear does not average. A steady stream of cars blends into a hum that people stop hearing; what registers is what stands out from it. Caltrans' acoustics manual says so directly: "intermittent truck peak noise levels are more objectionable than the continuous level of fan noise," and an intruding noise "considerably louder than the background or ambient... usually becomes objectionable." Its example of timing is the one every resident knows: "an automobile horn at 2:00 a.m. is more disturbing than the same noise in traffic at 5:00 p.m."

Here the sounds that stand out are single-vehicle events: an engine compression brake on the downgrade, an unmuffled exhaust. Ten cars carry the energy of one truck, but they arrive as hum; the event arrives as an event. A 24-hour average can hold steady while the loud events multiply, and averages are all that has ever been measured here. Events are what wake people.

Source: Caltrans Technical Noise Supplement, §2.2.1 (human reaction to sound)

The pavement is most of a car's noise, and nobody has measured this pavement

At freeway speed, tire on pavement can be three-quarters or more of a car's noise energy (less for trucks, where exhaust stacks matter), so the surface choice moves the number: Caltrans' long-running test on I-80 at Davis found open-graded asphalt 4 to 5 decibels quieter than what it replaced, still, after thirteen years. Caltrans qualifies that result to lower elevations and snow-free highways. The pavement through Truckee is grooved concrete with the ruts filled by polyester inlay, chosen for durability under chains; no published study characterizes what it sounds like, and the rehabilitation through town was approved under a categorical exemption, so no noise analysis exists for the segment where the town lives.

Sources: Caltrans Technical Noise Supplement, §7.3 · Caltrans quieter pavement investigation · Notice of Exemption, Truckee rehabilitation

The rules, and who holds them

Federal noise rules apply only when a highway is built or substantially expanded, so nothing obliges anyone to measure or abate the existing noise. Retrofit sound walls stopped being Caltrans' job in 1998; a local agency would have to sponsor one, none has, and the Town's general plan prohibits sound walls anyway, preferring berms and setbacks while formally encouraging Caltrans toward quieter surfacing. Vehicle noise itself is state law: every vehicle must have an adequate muffler, and an unmuffled compression brake is enforced as a muffler violation by the CHP. The nearest receiver-level study Caltrans has produced on this corridor is at Blue Canyon, thirty miles west, where one sound barrier of twenty-five evaluated met the cost test.

Sources: 23 CFR Part 772 · Caltrans noise protocol (Type II history) · Town of Truckee Safety and Noise Element, policies SN-8.4 and SN-8.10 · Vehicle Code § 27150 · Blue Canyon noise study

What is not yet known

The last 24-hour measurement of the freeway through Truckee was taken in 2004; the 2018 update touched it only in passing. Whether traffic growth, new pavement, and two decades have moved the number, nobody knows, because nobody has measured it. A continuous calibrated monitor near downtown, logging loud events and maxima as well as averages and publishing openly beside the traffic data this site already collects, would close the gap. This site intends to build one.
